# 29 文化培养与现实问题

## 短文

During a history and society discussion class, I began a long reading practice around school discussion links culture, public manners, and modern life. At first I did not fully understand why <u>cultivate</u>, <u>cultural</u>, and <u>current</u> were repeated so often, but the history teacher said that repeated words become remembered words. The teacher explained that if one article is too short, a student may see a word once and then lose it. If one article is longer, the same word can return in a new line, a new detail, and a new feeling. So our class started a longer passage built around debating manners, public life, and education. In that passage, the key words did not appear once. They appeared again and again until the page itself became a place of review.

On the first day, I copied <u>cultivate</u> into my notebook several times and read <u>cultivate</u> aloud several times. Then I did the same with <u>cultural</u> and <u>current</u>. This was not empty copying. Every time I wrote <u>cultivate</u>, I linked it to the main scene. Every time I wrote <u>cultural</u>, I linked it to a clear action. Every time I wrote <u>current</u>, I linked it to a standard that could be checked. When I went home, I met <u>cultivate</u>, <u>cultural</u>, and <u>current</u> again in the original passage, in my notes, and in the questions from class. That early repetition felt slow, but it began to give the words weight.

The second stage became more demanding because students think culture is only history, not daily behavior. I wanted to move quickly, but the history teacher asked us to slow down and notice how <u>custom</u> worked with <u>customer</u> and how <u>current</u> changed the whole situation. At first I thought one reading would be enough. It was not enough. On the first reading, I only saw the main story. On the second reading, I finally saw where <u>custom</u> was needed. On the third reading, I noticed that <u>customer</u> did not stand alone; it led toward <u>current</u>. Repetition made these links visible. Without repetition, the links would have stayed hidden.

A few days later, the key words began to feel less cold. <u>cultivate</u> no longer looked like a word from a distant list. <u>cultivate</u> had a place in the passage, a tone in my voice, and a role in my memory. The same thing happened to <u>cultural</u> and <u>current</u>. Because the passage was long, I met these words in calm description, in a small problem, in a teacher’s reminder, and in my own reflection. That variety mattered. Repetition is useful, but repetition inside changing sentences is even more useful. It lets a learner understand not only the word itself, but also how the word behaves when the meaning becomes deeper.

Our teacher then added a speaking round. We had to close the book and retell the passage with the same key words. When I retold the first half, I made sure to use <u>cultivate</u>, <u>cultural</u>, and <u>current</u> again. When I retold the second half, I used <u>custom</u>, <u>customer</u>, and <u>current</u> again. At first the retelling was not smooth, but that difficulty was useful. It showed me which words I had only recognized and which words I could really use. After two more rounds, the retelling became stronger. The repeated article helped me because the same structure returned each time, and the same words returned inside that structure.

I also noticed that repeated reading changed my confidence in class. Before this practice, I often understood a word for one minute and forgot it the next day. After this practice, I could explain why <u>cultivate</u> mattered in the scene, why <u>cultural</u> remained central, and why <u>current</u> was not just decoration. I could also explain how <u>custom</u> supported <u>customer</u> and how <u>current</u> marked an important turning point. Because the passage was long enough, I was not memorizing a disconnected list. I was following one complete line of thought. That line of thought gave each repeated word a stable home.

At home, I continued with a simple review cycle. In the morning, I read the article once for general meaning. At noon, I read it again and marked every line containing <u>cultivate</u> or <u>cultural</u>. In the evening, I read it once more and focused on <u>current</u>, <u>custom</u>, and <u>customer</u>. On the next day, I covered the Chinese meanings and tried to understand the page only from context. This long cycle taught me something important. A longer article gives repeated words enough room to breathe. A longer article lets <u>cultivate</u> return naturally, lets <u>cultural</u> return naturally, and lets <u>current</u> return naturally until the reader stops feeling surprise and starts feeling familiarity.

Another advantage of the long passage was emotional memory. Because the text stayed with one clear situation, I could remember not only the meaning of <u>cultivate</u> but also the feeling around <u>cultivate</u>. I remembered when <u>cultural</u> became necessary and when <u>current</u> became useful. I remembered how <u>custom</u> led to <u>customer</u> and how <u>current</u> helped solve the harder part. Emotional memory is not magic. It grows when the same words appear in a meaningful sequence. That is why repeated reading worked so much better than a quick word list for me. The article gave the words a story, and the story gave the words force.

By the end of the practice cycle, the result was clear: culture becomes something practical and visible. I still could not say that every line was easy, but I could say that the words were no longer strangers. <u>cultivate</u>, <u>cultural</u>, and <u>current</u> had become active parts of one usable memory. <u>custom</u>, <u>customer</u>, and <u>current</u> had become tools rather than decorations. When the teacher asked what helped most, I did not mention speed or clever tricks. I mentioned time, repetition, and context. I mentioned seeing the same word many times until it stayed in mind without force. That was the change I wanted from the beginning.

Now, when I review this article again, I still follow the same order. First, I read for general meaning. Second, I read only for the repeated key words. Third, I retell the main part and use <u>cultivate</u>, <u>cultural</u>, <u>current</u>, <u>custom</u>, and <u>customer</u> on purpose. Finally, I try to write two new sentences with <u>current</u> and <u>cultivate</u>. This final step shows whether repetition has become real knowledge. For me, the answer is often yes, and that is why I wanted a longer article rather than a short one. A longer article offers more returns, more reminders, and more chances for a difficult word to stay long enough to become familiar.

## 本文高中重点词

- <u>cultivate</u>：培养；耕作
- <u>cultural</u>：文化的
- <u>current</u>：当前的；水流
- <u>custom</u>：习俗；习惯
- <u>customer</u>：顾客
