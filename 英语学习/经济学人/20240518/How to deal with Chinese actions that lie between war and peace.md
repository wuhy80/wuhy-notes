**Two years** after Xi Jinping declared a “no limits” partnership with Vladimir Putin, and as the leaders meet again in Beijing on May 16th and 17th, the world is getting a good look at what China’s approach means in practice. It is not pretty. As we explain this week, Chinese firms are **sustaining** (维持) Mr Putin’s war in Ukraine by selling Russia items needed to make weapons. China’s **coastguard** (海岸警卫队) is **bullying** (欺凌) vessels in the South China Sea, enforcing its **bogus** (虚假的) territorial claims. And China’s **spies** (间谍) are allegedly **meddling** (干涉) in Britain and elsewhere.

Mr Xi’s **challenge** (挑战) to the world is more **subtle** (微妙的) than that posed by the **warmongering** (好战的) Mr Putin. Yet it is still a problem. He **craves** (渴望) a might-makes-right order, letting China do as it pleases. Its support for **pariah** (被遗弃的) states is meant to **defy** (反抗) and divide the West, while avoiding a direct clash. Its “bullying” in the South China Sea falls short of war, but is intended to weaken **foes** (敌人). China thinks these **tactics** (策略) can be **sustained** (维持) without tipping into conflict. The question for any country that supports global rules is how far to let Mr Xi go.
